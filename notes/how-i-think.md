# How I Think

I started noticing the same pattern in how I approached very different problems.

**The pattern:**

I show up to a problem as it actually is, not as it was first described to me. Name what's actually happening, even when that's not the version anyone asked for. Structure the ambiguity before anyone tells me to. Resist the pull to jump straight to a solution before the problem is real. Hand it off clean when it's done — not keep it, not become the permanent answer to it.

I don't wait to be assigned this. I don't wait for someone to notice I'm doing it, either. It's not a strategy I run — it's closer to just doing the thing the situation obviously needs, and it's been true for as long as I can remember working.

**What it actually looks like:**

It shows up in completely unrelated rooms in the same week — a status meeting, a scoping conversation, a governance discussion — different topics, same underlying move: nobody assigned the ambiguity to me, nobody handed me a mandate, but it was sitting there unresolved and I went and structured it anyway. That's the pattern showing up again. It isn't something I consciously turn on; it's closer to what my best work tends to look like.

**The thing I have to watch in myself:**

The same instinct that makes me good at this can also make me the person who quietly absorbs whatever ambiguity is lying around, whether or not it's actually mine to carry. High standards and a sense of responsibility cut both ways — they're why I can often find a path through uncertainty that has stayed unresolved, and they're also how I end up over-owning work that was never clearly mine to carry.

So the harder question isn't "can I make this work." The real question is whether the thing in front of me actually deserves the energy — not whether I *can* solve it, not what the situation *needs* from whoever's closest, but what's actually worth me spending myself on. That's a much smaller, harder filter than "am I capable of this," and I'm still learning to apply it before I say yes instead of after.

**Where the pull toward building fits in:**

I don't just want to diagnose the problem and hand someone a recommendation. I want to see if the thing I think is true actually holds up when it's built and someone real is using it. That's the bet underneath most of what I do — not being the person who produces the smartest analysis of what should happen, but the person who ships the smallest real version of it and lets it prove itself against actual use. Analysis that never becomes something usable doesn't interest me for very long.

That pattern — name it, structure it, build the smallest real version, hand it off clean — shows up the same whether the stakes are a messy piece of enterprise architecture or something small enough to matter to one other person. Scale changes. The pattern doesn't.
