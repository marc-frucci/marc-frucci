# How I Think

I started noticing the same pattern in how I approached very different problems.

**The pattern:**

I show up to a problem as it actually is, not as it was first described to me. Name what's actually happening, even when that's not the version anyone asked for. Structure the ambiguity before anyone tells me to. Resist the pull to jump straight to a solution before the problem is real. Hand it off clean when it's done, not keep it, not become the permanent answer to it.

I don't wait to be assigned this. I don't wait for someone to notice I'm doing it, either. It's not a strategy I run. It's closer to just doing the thing the situation obviously needs. It's shown up across most of the work I've done.

**What it actually looks like:**

It shows up in completely unrelated rooms in the same week (a status meeting, a scoping conversation, a governance discussion), different topics, same underlying move: nobody assigned the ambiguity to me, nobody handed me a mandate, but it was sitting there unresolved and I went and structured it anyway. It isn't something I consciously turn on; it's closer to what my best work tends to look like.

**The thing I have to watch in myself:**

The same instinct that makes me good at this can also make me absorb ambiguity that was never clearly mine to carry. High standards and a sense of responsibility cut both ways. They help me find a path through problems that have stayed unresolved, but they can also lead me to over-own the work.

The harder question isn't always whether I *can* make something work. It's whether the problem is important enough, and mine enough, to deserve the effort.

**Where the pull toward building fits in:**

I don't just want to diagnose the problem and hand someone a recommendation. I want to see if the thing I think is true actually holds up when it's built and someone real is using it. That's the bet underneath most of what I do. I'd rather see whether something holds up once someone is actually using it than stop at a recommendation. Analysis that never becomes something usable doesn't interest me for very long.

The problems vary in scale, but the underlying approach tends to stay the same: understand what's actually happening, make it tractable, build only what earns its place, and leave it usable without me.
