# How I Think

I started noticing the same pattern in how I approached very different problems.

**The pattern:**

Show up to a problem as it actually is, not as it was first described. Name what's actually happening, even when that's not the version anyone asked for. Structure the ambiguity before anyone asks for it to be structured. Resist the pull to jump straight to a solution before the problem is real. Hand it off clean when it's done: don't keep it, don't become the permanent answer to it.

**What it actually looks like:**

Kolten Kowalsky, who mentored me at NBCUniversal, described the pattern itself from the outside: *"He created value by operating as a self-starter in ambiguous situations, taking ownership with limited tactical guidance, pressure-testing assumptions, and translating complex inputs into practical paths forward."* That's one account of the move above, from someone who watched it happen, not just my own framing of it.

The clearest instances of this are enterprise work that isn't mine to publish (see [case-studies](../case-studies/README.md)), so the two I can point to directly are smaller: noticing my wife's craft-fair leads were getting lost to the format they were captured in and scoping a tool for exactly that ([CraftCatcher](../builds/craftcatcher/README.md)), and turning a recurring Sunday planning decision into a generated rotation instead of a blank page ([Dinner Draw](../builds/dinner-draw/README.md)). Different scale, same move: find the actual friction, then build only enough to remove it.

Keith Berecz, Director of Product at NBCUniversal, described one specific instance of it: *"He stepped in to work through a thorny set of challenges involving sensitive data. The work required navigating legal, operational, cybersecurity, engineering, product, and program management considerations. Marc brought the right people together, created clarity, and helped move us toward a thoughtful and workable path forward."*

**The thing I have to watch in myself:**

The same instinct that makes this useful can also tip into a problem: I'll take on ambiguity that was never clearly mine to carry, mistaking the ability to make something work for a reason I should be the one doing it.

The harder question isn't always whether I *can* make something work. It's whether the problem is important enough, and mine enough, to deserve the effort.

**Where the pull toward building fits in:**

I don't just want to diagnose the problem and hand someone a recommendation. I want to see if the thing I think is true actually holds up once it's built and someone real is using it. Analysis that never becomes something usable doesn't interest me for very long.

The problems vary in scale, but the underlying approach tends to stay the same: understand what's actually happening, make it tractable, build only what earns its place, and leave it usable without me.
